HappyChildren — one small thing to do with your child today

This is a parenting app for parents who don’t have time to be taught parenting. It gives you one thing to try with one of your children, something to say to open it, and then it closes. About forty seconds a day. You can tap through it on the left. What each screen is for is on the right.

    What the research actually supports

    Back and forth, not input

    Harvard’s Center on the Developing Child calls it serve and return. Your child reaches out, you answer, and that exchange is what builds the architecture. The answering is the active part, which is why every suggestion in here ends with you waiting instead of talking.

    The phone is what you’re competing with

    Research on technoference links a parent’s interrupted attention to more acting out, more anxiety and weaker executive function. In a UK survey of over 2,000 students, 32% said they feel unimportant when a parent gets distracted by a phone. Only 10% of parents thought their phone use bothered their kids at all.

    Five minutes, child-led

    Parent-Child Interaction Therapy puts five minutes a day of play the child leads at the center of its first phase, with the parent praising, reflecting, imitating and describing, and not questioning or correcting. It is the best-evidenced small piece of parenting time there is.

    Ratio, not intensity

    Gottman’s 5:1 — five positive exchanges for every corrective one — is why this counts small moments rather than scheduling big ones. A hug counts and a noticed effort counts. A planned day out is one thing that happened in a month.

    Repair is the skill

    Secure attachment doesn’t come from never losing it. Dan Siegel’s four S’s — safe, seen, soothed, secure — put getting back together after a fight at the center of it. So a bad night is treated here as an ordinary one with something to say afterward.

    Getting people to keep using it is the real problem

    In a large trial of a digital parenting program, 97% of caregivers finished the first module and 8% finished all twelve. Across smartphone interventions generally, completion runs as low as 0.5%. Any feature that assumes a parent will work through a curriculum is a feature nobody will use.

    What it deliberately doesn’t do

    × Streaks and badges

    A broken streak on a parenting app says you failed your child. Guilt gets apps deleted, and the parents having the worst month are the ones it lands on.

    × A content library

    Articles, courses and modules are what most parenting apps fill up with, and they are what people stop opening. One card a day, or nothing.

    × Child accounts or monitoring

    No child signs in and no child is tracked. That keeps children’s data out of this entirely, and it keeps COPPA off the critical path. The 2025 rules tightened consent, retention and vendor liability, and collecting nothing is the cheapest way to comply.

    × Screen-time shaming

    Tracking dashboards mostly get swiped away. This blocks the phone for a few minutes with one of your children’s names on it, which is a reason to put it down.

    × An AI chat companion

    Sending a description of your child to a model is the riskiest thing this could do. It also swaps the thing that works, which is you talking to your child, for you talking to a phone.

    × A fixed daily reminder

    Seven at night is bath time in some houses and bedtime in others. The prompt comes once, inside a window you set, and it doesn’t come again.

    If you build it, build this first

    Version one

    Screens 2, 3, 4 and 6 — add your children, one card each per day, the moment itself, and the eight lines. All the content ships inside the app, so there is no server, no account and no login. Two or three weeks for one developer.

    Version two

    Noticed, and the child profile. That is where people coming back comes from, and it is the first part worth asking anyone to pay for.

    The honest part

    Parenting apps mostly fail, and not on the product. Two things decide this one: whether the daily card is good enough that a parent screenshots it, and whether the app really can be closed in forty seconds. Both are worth testing with twenty parents before anybody writes a second screen.
