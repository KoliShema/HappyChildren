    What the research actually supports

    Back-and-forth, not input

    Harvard's Center on the Developing Child calls it serve and return: a child reaches out, an adult responds, and that exchange builds brain architecture. The response is the active ingredient — which is why every moment in the app ends with waiting rather than talking.

    The phone is the competition

    "Technoference" research links parents' interrupted attention to more acting out, more anxiety and weaker executive function in children. In a UK survey of over 2,000 pupils, 32% said they feel unimportant when a parent is distracted by a phone — while only 10% of parents thought their phone use upset their kids.

    Five minutes, child-led

    Parent-Child Interaction Therapy builds its entire first phase on five minutes a day of play the child leads, with the parent praising, reflecting, imitating, describing — and not questioning or correcting. It is the best-evidenced small unit of parenting time there is.

    Ratio over intensity

    Gottman's 5:1 principle — five positive exchanges for every corrective one — is why this app counts small moments instead of scheduling big ones. A hug and a noticed effort are units. A planned day out is not.

    Repair is the skill

    Secure attachment doesn't come from never rupturing. Dan Siegel's four S's — safe, seen, soothed, secure — put reconnection after conflict at the centre. So the app treats "you lost your temper" as a normal Tuesday with a script attached, not a failure state.

    Engagement is the real problem

    In a large trial of a twelve-module digital parenting programme, 97% finished module one and 8% finished all twelve. Across smartphone interventions generally, completion runs as low as 0.5%. Any feature that assumes a parent will work through a curriculum is a feature that won't get used.

    What it deliberately doesn't do

    × Streaks and badges

    A broken streak on a parenting app reads as "you failed your child." Guilt drives uninstalls, and the parents who need this most are the ones having the worst weeks.

    × A content library

    Articles, courses and modules are where parenting apps go to die. One card a day, or nothing.

    × Child accounts or monitoring

    No child logs in and no child is tracked. That keeps children's data out of the system and COPPA off the critical path — the 2025 rules tightened third-party consent, retention and vendor liability, and the cheapest compliance is collecting nothing.

    × Screen-time shaming

    Tracking dashboards mostly get dismissed; friction and blocking change behaviour. The app blocks the phone for five named minutes with a child's name on it, which is a reason rather than a scold.

    × An AI chat companion

    Routing a parent's description of their child into a model is the riskiest thing this app could do, and it swaps the thing that works — the parent talking to the child — for the parent talking to a phone.

    × A fixed daily reminder

    7pm is bathtime in some houses and bedtime in others. The prompt arrives once, inside a window the parent sets, and never twice.

    If you build it, build this first

    Version one

    Screens 2, 3, 4 and 6 — add children, one card each per day, the moment detail, and the eight scripts. All content ships inside the app; no server, no account, no login. Two to three weeks of one developer's time.

    Version two

    Noticed (7) and the child profile (8). This is where retention comes from, and the first thing worth asking people to pay for.

    The honest risk

    Parenting apps are a graveyard. The two things that decide this one are whether the daily card is good enough that parents screenshot it, and whether the app is genuinely closable in forty seconds. Test both with twenty parents before writing the second screen.
